FastSpeech 2
English and Telugu
Implementation, training and test-sentence analysis
Parichay Mago | Technical report | 4 November 2026
Abstract
This work develops two independent FastSpeech 2 text-to-speech systems for English and Telugu using
Svarah and IndicTTS Telugu. We began by examining the reference architecture, checking dataset
access and transcript quality, and selecting approximately five hours of training audio and one hour of
validation audio per language. We then implemented language-specific text processing, phoneme
conversion, forced alignment and acoustic-feature extraction. Separate acoustic models predict mel
spectrograms, which a pretrained HiFi-GAN converts into speech [1-5]. The experiments investigate
training convergence, alignment quality and English prosody through baseline training, feature repair
and targeted fine-tuning. Evaluation covers all ten assignment sentences and their predicted duration,
pitch and energy trajectories.
Implementation overview
The first implementation stage established the data pipeline. We checked usable audio duration, built
reproducible train/validation splits and handled speaker conditioning. English required estimated
acoustic speaker labels because original speaker IDs were unavailable in the audited snapshot. Telugu
required a pronunciation lexicon and a stable phone mapping compatible with the reference
implementation.
We next trained separate English and Telugu models using aligned duration, pitch and energy targets.
Inspection of the initial synthesized speech led to an alignment audit: omitted internal gaps had
shortened acoustic targets. We repaired the feature extractor, rebuilt both languages' features and
trained fresh models while preserving the earlier experiments.
The later English experiments addressed remaining clarity and pacing concerns. A low-learning-rate
fine-tune was followed by an extension with word-boundary and punctuation embeddings and learned
pause-presence and pause-duration predictors. Finally, we selected checkpoints using complete
validation evaluations, generated the required test audios and analysed their variance predictions
alongside the loss curves.
The report follows an introduction-method-experiments-discussion structure, informed by the Seed-TTS technical report [11].
Methods and data appear on pp. 2-5, experimental results on pp. 6-9, sentence-level analysis on pp. 10-19, and discussion,
reproducibility, corrections, speaker coverage and references on pp. 20-25.

1. Introduction and dataset preparation
Adapting a text-to-speech system to two languages requires a consistent path from written text to
pronunciation, acoustic timing and waveform generation. Here, the main implementation challenges
were speaker conditioning in English, phoneme representation in Telugu and reliable alignment-based
supervision in both languages. The objective was to build and evaluate two separate systems from the
reference FastSpeech 2 implementation [1,2].
The assignment specifies two separate models, approximately five hours of training audio and one hour
of validation per language, the exact five test sentences per language, loss curves, and
duration/F0/energy analysis for every test. E2a/E2b and T2a/T2b count separately, giving ten required
audios and thirty variance figures.

1.2 Subsampling and split separation
Seed 20261102 determines an ordering of atomic groups. Validation is selected first, then training,
greedily approaching each duration target. Groups connect repeated normalized transcripts, identical
audio hashes and English chunks from the same recording. No group crosses splits. The selected Telugu
subset uses the source male label. These are held-out utterance/recording splits, not speaker-disjoint
evaluation.
1.3 Speaker conditioning
The audited Svarah snapshot lacked recoverable original speaker IDs. Training-only average-linkage
cosine clustering of pretrained SpeechBrain ECAPA embeddings, with heuristic distance threshold 0.30,
produced 254 estimated labels. Validation uses frozen training centroids. These labels can fragment or
merge voices; they are not verified people. All final English tests retain en_est_0155 from earlier
diagnostics. Label coverage and selection limits are quantified in Appendix D. Telugu uses the source
label te_male, without independently verified identity [9].

Separate acoustic models. Speaker conditioning; English adds text-derived boundary embeddings and pause heads.
The reference variant encodes phones with feed-forward Transformer blocks, predicts duration, pitch and
energy, length-regulates the sequence, and decodes mel spectrograms. A convolutional postnet refines
mels. This project uses direct phoneme-level F0, not the paper's later CWT representation, and an
external HiFi-GAN. It does not implement the FastSpeech 2s direct waveform decoder [1-3].

English learned-pause extension
Each word has a boundary slot, including words with zero measured pause. A seven-entry boundary
embedding conditions the encoder; two new variance predictors estimate pause presence and positive
pause length. Punctuation categories distinguish unpunctuated, comma, period, question, exclamation
and clause boundaries. The parent acoustic weights are loaded exactly; new heads receive a larger
learning rate. The existing pitch and energy heads also receive the new encoder context.
At inference a sigmoid threshold 0.5 selects a positive pause, its predicted log duration becomes rounded
frames, and the length regulator generates the acoustic span. Terminal boundary duration is zero by
design; it supplies sentence context, not trailing silence. The safety cap 354 frames is the
training-observed maximum, not a hand-set punctuation pause. Lexical durations have a one-frame floor.
Telugu adaptation
The Telugu path preserves a bijective IPA-to-ASCII phone map, its own src/lexicon/alignment model,
statistics and checkpoint. Unknown phones are rejected. Telugu uses the base variance adaptor; no
learned word-boundary/pause heads were added. The models share a frozen vocoder, not acoustic
parameters or optimizers.

2.1 Preprocessing and alignment repair
Text, audio and alignment
English normalizes text through the reference cleaners and g2p-en ARPAbet conversion. Telugu uses
NFC, number verbalization and eSpeak-NG Telugu pronunciations, mapped to stable lowercase telph
identifiers. English training pronunciations cover 6,470 words; Telugu covers 13,848. English symbol IDs
remain stable when Telugu symbols are appended [7,8].
Original English source is 16 kHz mono; the selected Telugu source is 48 kHz mono. scipy resample_poly
creates 22,050 Hz PCM16 copies. Original WAVs and raw transcripts remain unchanged. MFA 3.3.9
supplies alignments: English uses english_us_arpa with speaker adaptation disabled; Telugu fits an
acoustic aligner using training audio only, then freezes it for validation. Telugu alignment is not the TTS
model itself [6].
Repairing omitted alignment gaps
An earlier extraction skipped blank intervals or timestamp gaps, shortening acoustic targets and
compromising duration supervision. The corrected gap-fixed-v2 features insert silence tokens for real
gaps, preserve the complete cropped timeline, and enforce sum(duration frames)=mel length. Both
language models were then trained afresh on corrected features; old checkpoints remain separate.

Feature extraction and normalization
WORLD DIO plus StoneMask estimates F0; interpolated F0 and frame energy are averaged over phones.
Spectral energy is the linear L2 norm. Pitch/energy mean, standard deviation and bins are fitted on
training features only, with per-example IQR filtering for fitting; validation uses frozen parameters.
Aligned edge silence is cropped; internal gaps are retained. Arrays must be finite, known-phone
compatible and length-consistent.
For the English pause pilot, all 3,654 corrected usable clips are retained. Every real gap has its measured
frame target; absent gaps have zero. Lexical phone durations/pitch/energy, source mel bytes, split
membership, speaker mapping and normalization statistics are unchanged. This prevents fabricated
pauses from becoming supervision.

Executed runner arguments override the upstream batch 16 / 900k-step template. Training GPUs were
RTX PRO 4500 Blackwell for the corrected paired run, A40 for the fine-tune, and RTX 3090 for the pause
pilot; PyTorch 2.8.0 with CUDA 12.8 and Python 3.10 were used. Final inference and this analysis run
locally on CPU.
Base objective: five loss terms
Lbase = Lmel + Lpostnet + Lpitch + Lenergy + Llog_duration
Mel terms use L1 error; pitch, energy and log(1+duration) use MSE. Six numbers are logged: total
followed by the five component losses. No claim of six separate component terms is intended.
Learned-pause objective: seven loss terms
Lpause = Lmel + Lpostnet + Lpitch + Lenergy + Lspeech_duration
+ 0.25 * Lpresence + 0.25 * Lpositive_pause_duration
Pause presence uses weighted BCE, with training-only positive weight 2.9192; positive pause length uses
log-duration MSE. Speech-duration loss excludes boundary slots; pitch/energy losses exclude tokens
without target frames. Eight numbers are logged including total. Because both terms and masks change,
base and pause totals are not directly comparable.
Validation interpretation
Only complete held-out evaluations select best checkpoints. Batch losses are averaged with
example-count weights; they are not global token-weighted corpus sums. Validation decoder mels use
target duration/pitch/energy while their predictor errors are still recorded. This teacher-forced evaluation
does not establish free-running waveform quality. Training minibatch losses have dropout and variable
content, so their gap to validation is only suggestive.

Conclusions
The system is operational, source-preserving and reproducible for the ten tests. It predicts nonconstant
phoneme-level variance, uses frontend-supplied conduct stress and changes pattu predictions across
contexts. Validation plateaus favor earlier checkpoints. English pause F1 is 45.6% versus 38.5% and
21.0% for simple occurrence baselines; selected pause-duration MAE is slightly worse than initialization.
These results establish limited predictor-level gains, while audible prosody improvement remains
unproven.
Limitations and future work
The author accepted all ten final recordings and found both languages acceptable, with no specific
pronunciation issues noticed. This single author review supplies no detailed phonetic notes or quality
ranking. Follow with independent, language-competent per-test review, preserving concrete observations
separately from automated scores. Audit OOV and number/cross-language pronunciations, especially
E1/E3/E4 and T1/T3/T4. Improve speaker-label confidence and coherent voice coverage before assuming
more English steps will fix clarity. Rebalance informative punctuation examples and validate occurrence
calibration and pause timing independently; held-out clause/exclamation coverage is currently
insufficient.
Only after those checks should another bounded pilot be considered. T4 has identical phonemes and
all-zero boundary inputs with or without its comma: Telugu punctuation cannot condition the current
acoustic output. Fixing this requires consistent training and inference supervision. Finer pitch
trajectories, a voiced/unvoiced model or a better-matched vocoder are also unmeasured future
experiments. Appendix D quantifies speaker sparsity and the remaining quality-evaluation limits.
